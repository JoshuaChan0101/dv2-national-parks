Life inside the fence

Australia protects some of its best wildlife habitat as national parks. This page compares 12 of them, from the tropical north to Tasmania: how many species each holds, which threatened animals they shelter, and when visitors spot them.

Which parks hold the most wildlife?

Kakadu holds the most wildlife of the 12 parks, with 551 species recorded. Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair holds the fewest, with 158. “Species recorded” means the number of different birds, mammals, reptiles and frogs that people have reported seeing in the park since 2000. It is not a full count of everything that lives there. More records can also mean more visitors: a park that is easy to reach and busy with people looking will log more species.

Bigger parks do hold more species, but only a little more. The trend line climbs slowly: a park ten times larger holds about 1.5 times as many species. Daintree, the Blue Mountains and Wilsons Promontory sit well above the line, so they hold more species than their size suggests. Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair and Karijini sit well below it. Ikara-Flinders Ranges sits right on the line.

Who lives where?

The records cover four animal groups: birds, mammals, reptiles and frogs. Birds make up the largest share in every park, because they are the easiest to spot. The mix still differs from park to park. Uluru-Kata Tjuta is strong in birds, with 264 species, but weak in mammals, with only 16. It was also a surprise to find so much life in the dry middle of the continent: 356 species in total, more than the Grampians or Wilsons Promontory, and more reptiles (69) than the Blue Mountains.

Why protected areas matter

A species is called threatened when it is at risk of dying out. Australia’s national list has three levels: Vulnerable (a high risk), Endangered (a very high risk) and Critically Endangered (an extremely high risk). National parks matter because they give these animals safe habitat to live and breed in. Each square below is one threatened species recorded in a park. The Blue Mountains, Daintree, Kakadu and Wilsons Promontory record the most critically endangered species.

Kakadu comes out on top for four of the five measures: the most species, the most sightings, the most threatened species and the largest area. But it holds the fewest species for each square kilometre. Freycinet is the opposite: the smallest park, but the most species for its size. Among the smaller parks, Wilsons Promontory stands out for threatened animals, with 25 recorded.

When do visitors see wildlife?

Most sightings are logged by visitors and citizen scientists, who record what they see in apps and on websites, along with some researchers. So these charts show when and where people look, as well as where wildlife lives. Use them to plan a visit: choose a park for the animal you hope to see, then pick the darkest month for that park below. Parks in the north and inland, such as Kakadu, Kalbarri, Karijini and Ikara-Flinders Ranges, peak in the cooler months of July and August. Freycinet, Wilsons Promontory and Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair peak over summer, and Daintree, the Grampians, Flinders Chase and Uluru-Kata Tjuta peak in October.

Notes

  • parks.csv (from CAPAD 2024 Terrestrial): kept only the national-park rows for the 12 parks and added up all pieces of each park. Area is the gazetted area, or the area calculated from the boundary where the gazetted figure was 0. The map point is the centre of each park’s largest piece. States are written in full and columns are trimmed to park, state, area, latitude and longitude.
  • park_species.csv and observations.csv (from ALA records): one download per park, clipped to the CAPAD boundary. Boundaries were simplified for 10 parks, and Daintree and the Blue Mountains were downloaded as square tiles and then clipped to the exact boundary, so a few records near park edges may be included or missed. Only birds, mammals, reptiles and frogs from 2000 onwards are kept. Records not identified to species were dropped, subspecies were merged into their species, and each species has one common name. Two Indigenous-language names were replaced with plain English names (Smoky Mouse, Northern Quoll). The time charts leave out 2026, which is an incomplete year.
  • Conservation status comes from ALA’s national status field and is applied park by park. Animals listed only at subspecies level are not counted as threatened. Two doubtful cases were checked on SPRAT: the Pilotbird is listed, so it was kept, and the Tiger Snake was set to not threatened, because only Krefft’s Tiger Snake in the Flinders Ranges is listed.
  • states.topojson: the state boundaries from the source above, converted to TopoJSON with mapshaper. Used only for the map background.
  • Limits: records show where people look, so busy parks log more. ALA hides the exact locations of some sensitive species.

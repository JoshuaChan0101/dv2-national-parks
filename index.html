Life inside the fence

In Australia, many of the national parks are protecting the country's unique wildlife areas. Different national parks are compared here as regards how many animal species they are home to, what endangered animals live in these parks and when people see these animals.

Which parks hold the most wildlife?

Kakadu is the national park with the most amount of wildlife - it is home to 551 animal species - while Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair has the least wildlife, which is represented with only 158 different species. “Species recorded” means the number of different birds, mammals, reptiles and frogs that people have reported seeing in the park since 2000. However, this is not the total information about the number of species living in the national parks. Thus, more records can tell about a higher amount of visitors coming to a national park.

Larger parks do support a greater number of species, but the difference is marginal. The increase in species is proportional with the size of the park (the bigger the park, the more species can be found), but not very often. Daintree, Blue Mountains, and Wilsons Promontory are examples of parks that outperform their size. For instance, Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair and Karijini do not meet the expected number of species for their size of the park, while Ikara-Flinders Ranges are right on the predicted level.

Who lives where?

The data includes four animal groups: birds, mammals, reptiles, and frogs. Birds represent the most populous group present in every park. However, there is a difference in the number of species among parks. For example, Uluru-Kata Tjuta has a high number of birds (264) and a low number of mammals (16). Additionally, it is impressive that there is such a large number of species found in the arid central region of Australia (356 species in total).

Why protected areas matter

When a species is on the verge of extinction, it is generally referred to as threatened. The Australian national listing contains these three categories: Vulnerable, Endangered, and Critically Endangered. With national parks, the importance lies in the fact that these animals are able to find adequate living and breeding grounds. Each square below represents one threatened species recorded in the park.

Kakadu comes out on top for four of the five measures: the most species, the most sightings, the most threatened species and the largest area. Nonetheless, it has the least number of species’ sightings per square kilometer. Freycinet is completely opposite: it has the smallest area, but an array of species. Among the smaller parks, Wilsons Promontory is the most noticeable due to the number of threatened animals as the number of them there is 25.

When do visitors see wildlife?

Though most of these records are being made by visitors and citizen scientists on apps and websites, there are also a small number of researchers who record them. Thus, these charts basically track when and where people look and where wildlife lives. Use them to plan your visit: pick out a park for the animal you hope to see, and then select the darkest month for that park below. Parks further north and inland, such as Kakadu, Kalbarri, Karijini, and Ikara-Flinders Ranges, reach a peak in July and August in the cooler months. Freycinet, Wilsons Promontory, and Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair peak over summer, and Daintree, Grampians, Flinders Chase, and Uluru-Kata Tjuta peak in October.

Notes

  • parks.csv (from CAPAD 2024 Terrestrial): kept only the national-park rows for the 12 parks and added up all pieces of each park. Area is the gazetted area, or the area calculated from the boundary where the gazetted figure was 0. The map point is the centre of each park’s largest piece. States are written in full and columns are trimmed to park, state, area, latitude and longitude.
  • park_species.csv and observations.csv (from ALA records): one download per park, clipped to the CAPAD boundary. Boundaries were simplified for 10 parks, and Daintree and the Blue Mountains were downloaded as square tiles and then clipped to the exact boundary, so a few records near park edges may be included or missed. Only birds, mammals, reptiles and frogs from 2000 onwards are kept. Records not identified to species were dropped, subspecies were merged into their species, and each species has one common name. Two Indigenous-language names were replaced with plain English names (Smoky Mouse, Northern Quoll). The time charts leave out 2026, which is an incomplete year.
  • Conservation status comes from ALA’s national status field and is applied park by park. Animals listed only at subspecies level are not counted as threatened. Two doubtful cases were checked on SPRAT: the Pilotbird is listed, so it was kept, and the Tiger Snake was set to not threatened, because only Krefft’s Tiger Snake in the Flinders Ranges is listed.
  • states.topojson: the state boundaries from the source above, converted to TopoJSON with mapshaper. Used only for the map background.
  • Limits: records show where people look, so busy parks log more. ALA hides the exact locations of some sensitive species.
